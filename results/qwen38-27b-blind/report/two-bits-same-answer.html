Two Bits, Same Answer

A 2-bit quantisation of Qwen3.8-27B finished 96 of 225 coding tasks. So did a 4-bit one. They did not finish the same 96 — a quarter of the benchmark went to exactly one of them — and the 2-bit file spent about a third more energy, which is the one difference here that is statistically resolved.

Both arms ran the full 225-exercise aider polyglot benchmark on one RTX 3090 with identical settings. Power telemetry covers all 225 exercises of the second arm and 132 of the first, whose sampler was started after its benchmark — every energy figure below is paired over exercises both arms recorded.

Two files of the same model, differing only in how tightly the weights are packed. The smaller one frees 4.4 GB of video memory — the difference between fitting on a card and not. The question a buyer actually has is what that costs.

The equal totals hide the more interesting fact. Only 66 exercises were solved by both arms. Fifty-nine — a quarter of the benchmark — were solved by exactly one of them, split almost evenly at 29 and 30. So the matching scores are substantially coincidence: the two files succeed on materially different tasks and happen to land on the same total. The pass rates are indistinguishable, which is not the same as equal — the interval spans thirteen points, and a real difference anywhere inside it fits this data.

What is resolved is the cost. Its confidence interval excludes 1.0, so unlike the accuracy difference the energy difference is a measured result rather than a null. The 2-bit file needs about a fifth more tokens to reach the same answer, takes about a fifth longer, and burns roughly a third more energy per task it actually completes.

Per token, the smaller file is the more efficient one. It decodes at 77 tok/s against 70, and costs 6.66 J per completion token against 6.92 — about 4% better. It then needs 20% more tokens, which more than cancels the gain. Efficiency reverses depending on whether the denominator is a token or a finished task, and only the second one is what a user pays.

The first-attempt gap is the interesting one. The 2-bit file gets it right first time far less often — 8.4% against 13.3% — and then recovers to exactly the same second-attempt rate. It is not less capable at these tasks so much as less likely to land them cleanly, and the retry is where the extra tokens and energy go.

The token-overhead measurement is borrowed from Quesma's August 2026 study, which reported roughly 1.25× output tokens for a 2-bit file against BF16. Measured here against a 4-bit anchor instead, and paired over only the exercises both arms solved — so an arm cannot look efficient by quietly solving an easier subset.

Drafter off, the same file runs 45.2 tok/s — 644 GB/s, or 69% of the roof, which is bandwidth-bound. Turn the draft head on and measured throughput exceeds the one-weight-pass-per-token ceiling of 65.7 tok/s, so that model cannot explain it: real traffic is 398 GB/s and the memory system has 57% of its capacity spare. What binds instead is the 350 W board limit, active on essentially every busy sample.

The cap is paid in core clock, not memory clock

Reading both clocks against their own maxima explains why the bandwidth roof stays put while achievable throughput falls short of it: the SM clock is held at 1650 of 2130 MHz (77%) while the memory clock sits at 9501 of 9751 MHz (97%). The power limit lowers the compute roof and leaves the bandwidth roof untouched. Power is being spent holding a clock this workload is not using — an unexploited firmware lever, and one this rig cannot test, because llama.cpp cannot express a clock policy and the driver does not expose one.

Cooling is not the constraint, but there is no headroom either. Thermal limiting fires on 3% of busy samples while the fan runs at 100% and the die sits at 83 °C median. A better cooler buys perhaps 3%; the current operating point already costs the entire fan curve. Memory junction temperature is not exposed by NVML on this part and is absent for that reason, not by omission.

04 — RetractionsClaims this campaign published and then killed

Dead claims are kept as dated case studies rather than deleted. Each of these was measured, published, and then refuted by a better measurement — in every case one this campaign ran against its own work.

Corrected — a true number that read as the wrong claim

A 2-bit quantisation solved the same number of coding tasks as a 4-bit one.

True as arithmetic, misleading as a claim. Both finished 96 of 225 — but only 66 were solved by both, and 59 by exactly one, split 29 to 30. The equal totals conceal disagreement on a quarter of the benchmark, and the sentence invited the reading that the two files are interchangeable here. They score alike in aggregate; they do not succeed on the same work.

Corrected — energy compared across different exercises

The 2-bit file costs 1.52× the energy per solved task: 23.2 kJ against 15.3 kJ.

Paired properly: 1.32×. The first arm's power sampler was started after its benchmark, so it has telemetry for 132 of 223 exercises while the second has 222. The cost table averaged each arm over whatever it happened to have — different exercises — and divided tokens over a third set again. Recomputed over only the exercises where both arms have power, the ratio is 1.376× on total energy, 1.239× as a per-exercise median, and 1.319× per solved task. The direction survived; the magnitude was overstated by about 15%.

Retracted — drafter depth

Acceptance sits at 89% of the configured cap and stays 59% at the last permitted position, so the flag and not the draft head limits speculation; raising it should be worth about +24%.

Measured: −0.8% at n-max 6, +2.0% at n-max 8. Mean accepted length rose exactly as predicted, 2.71 → 3.54, and throughput did not follow. The cost model assumed a constant marginal cost per speculated token; deeper drafts cost more per token and are accepted less often — 99/73/55/44%, decaying to 13% by position 7 — so wasted work grows faster than accepted work. A boundary acceptance rate says nothing about the marginal return of the next position.

Retracted — energy spread

Energy per completion token varies 30× across exercises.

Measured correctly: 3.2×. The window was anchored to the results-file timestamp minus the recorded duration — but that duration covers only model calls, while the file is written after the unit tests and the build cleanup. Right length, wrong place: it billed test-time idle to the model. The cheapest exercise was published at 0.417 J/token against a true 5.830.

Retracted — polling bias

A poll-derived phase split overstates the long phase by 6.3 points.

Measured over the full arm: −1.5 points — wrong in magnitude and in sign. The earlier figure came from a four-minute window dominated by start-up transients. Splits already published stand. The lesson kept is that a bias estimated on a short window is not a small version of the long-window bias, and may not even point the same way.

Retracted — thermal headroom

Thermal throttling never fires on this part.

It fires on 3.0% of busy samples. The earlier reading counted idle samples, which are 14% of the trace and swamped the histogram. Idle is a state, not a limit.

Corrected — uncertainty understated

With 59 discordant pairs, a difference of up to 1.3 points remains consistent.

The true interval is about five times wider. The rule of three is the bound for zero discordant pairs and is the wrong instrument once there are any. The paired comparison at the top of this page now carries a proper confidence interval. Understating uncertainty is the one direction this campaign must never round.

05 — LimitsWhat this rig cannot say

Stated plainly so absence is never read as a result.

  • No full-precision anchor. The quantisation ladder is referenced to UD-IQ4_XS — itself a lossy 4-bit file — because 55 GB of BF16 does not fit a 24 GB card, and 29 GB of Q8_0 would put roughly 7 GB of weights on a CPU with 31.8 GB of system RAM. Borrowing Quesma's published BF16 point bounds the anchor at at least 1.1% of tokens away from full precision — a lower bound, which cannot prove it is close.
  • Wall power is not measured. Every watt on this page is the NVML GPU board domain. Power supply, CPU, memory, drives and display are excluded.
  • Reasoning is off throughout. Other people's work finds the gap between quantisations widens at higher reasoning effort, so this is one corner of that surface. A full sweep is four more arms and fourteen hours of exclusive machine time.
  • One machine, one runtime, one context length. No second GPU vendor, no second inference engine, and 32k rather than the ~98k an agentic benchmark can demand.
  • Per-process GPU attribution is impossible here. nvidia-smi pmon reports nothing per process under Windows WDDM, so the server's share cannot be separated from the desktop's.

The practical read. If 4.4 GB of video memory is what stands between the model fitting and not fitting, the 2-bit file costs about a fifth more tokens and roughly a third more energy per finished task, at a pass rate this sample cannot distinguish. If the 4-bit file already fits, there is no measured reason to move down. And the two files are not interchangeable task-for-task even where their totals match — swapping one for the other changes which quarter of the work succeeds.

Instrumentation tier: in-band GPU board power only. Power supply, CPU, system memory, drives and display are excluded and unmeasured — nothing here may be called system power.
